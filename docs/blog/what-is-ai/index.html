What is AI, actually?

Strip away the hype and every computer program is the same thing: a list of rules that turns input into output. A calculator takes two numbers and applies the rule "add them." A spam filter takes an email and applies millions of small rules that end in "spam" or "not spam."

The only interesting question — and the question that separates normal software from AI — is: where did the rules come from?

Rules written by a person

In normal software, a human writes every rule. "If the password is wrong, show an error. If the cart is empty, disable the checkout button." This works well when a person can actually describe what to do. It fails badly when they can't.

Think about how you'd write rules for "recognize a cat in a photo." A cat can be black, orange, fluffy, hairless, in shadow, half behind a sofa. Nobody can write that rulebook. Not because people don't understand cats — because people understand cats in a way they can't put into words.

Rules learned from examples

AI is the shortcut around that wall: instead of a person writing the rules, a program finds the rules by looking at millions of examples of cats and adjusting itself until it stops being wrong. The programmer never writes what a cat is; the programmer builds the process that learns it.

That's the whole definition. AI is software whose rules were learned from examples instead of written by hand. Everything else — chatbots, image generators, self-driving cars, AI agents — is that same idea applied to a harder problem than cats.

The one-sentence test. Ask: could a person write the rulebook for this task? If yes, you don't need AI — a normal program will be cheaper, faster, and more predictable. If no (recognizing faces, understanding language, writing text), that's where AI earns its place.

Why the rules are hard to see

There's a consequence worth knowing early: learned rules are a tangle of numbers, not sentences. In a normal program you can open the code and read what it will do. In a trained model there is nothing to read — billions of small numbers that together produce the behavior. Nobody, including the people who built it, can point at a line and say "this is why it did that."

That single fact explains a lot of what you'll meet later in this series: why models sometimes behave strangely, why they have to be tested by using them rather than by reading them, and why they can be surprising even to their creators.

Where this is going

Chatbots feel like talking to a person, but the same definition applies: the rules for how words follow each other were learned from enormous amounts of human writing instead of written by linguists. The next lesson looks at that "brain" — what a model actually is — because almost everything else about AI agents stands on top of it.
